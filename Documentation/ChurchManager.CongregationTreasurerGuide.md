# ChurchManager congregation treasurer guide

**Maintained guidance for ChurchManager treasurers**

**Last reviewed:** August 18, 2026

> This guide supports good recordkeeping and consistent ChurchManager use. It
> is not tax or legal advice. Congregational policy and unusual gifts should be
> reviewed with a qualified tax or legal adviser.

## Directed gifts, designated purposes, and gifts for individuals

Small congregations must distinguish between:

1. a gift to an approved congregational purpose; and
2. money directed by the giver to a particular individual.

That distinction affects whether the congregation is acting as a charitable
organization or merely passing a personal gift from one person to another.

### The practical rule

A donor may support a congregation-approved charitable purpose, but the
congregation must retain full control and discretion over how the money is used
and who receives assistance.

The IRS explains that contributions must be made to or for the use of a
qualified organization and not set aside for a specific person. A contribution
earmarked for a particular person or family is generally not deductible as a
charitable contribution. See [IRS Publication 526, Charitable
Contributions](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526), especially
“Contributions to Individuals.”

IRS materials discussing earmarked gifts also emphasize the qualified
organization's control and discretion over the donated funds. See [IRS Private
Letter Ruling 2005-30-016](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-wd/0530016.pdf). A private
letter ruling applies only to the taxpayer who requested it, but its discussion
and cited authorities help explain the control-and-discretion principle. It is
not a substitute for advice concerning a congregation's particular situation.

### Student Support example

The congregation may establish an approved **Student Support** ministry and
select the student to receive support through its own authorized process.
Members may then give to Student Support because:

- the congregation approved the charitable purpose;
- the congregation selects the recipient;
- the congregation controls the fund; and
- the congregation retains authority to change the recipient, amount, timing,
  or use in furtherance of the approved purpose.

The donor is supporting the congregation's Student Support ministry. The donor
is not purchasing a pass-through payment to a chosen student.

If a donor writes “Student Support for Jane Doe” or otherwise insists that a
particular individual receive the payment, do not silently remove the name and
record the gift as an ordinary contribution.

### A new purpose that the congregation has not approved

A gift marked “for trees on the church property” is different from a gift for a
named individual. Trees on church property can be a legitimate congregational
purpose, but the donor is proposing a restricted use that the congregation has
not yet accepted.

Do not automatically classify the gift as nondeductible merely because the
congregation had not previously planned to plant trees. Instead, hold it for an
authorized decision:

1. The congregation may accept the restricted gift and approve the tree project
   if it is willing and able to carry it out as part of its purposes.
2. The congregation may ask the donor for permission to use the gift for a
   broader approved purpose, such as property improvements or the general fund.
3. If the congregation will not accept the restriction and the donor will not
   redesignate the gift, return it.

The donor cannot unilaterally force the congregation to undertake the project by
sending money. Conversely, the congregation should not accept a restricted gift
and then knowingly spend it for something else. Acceptance, redesignation, or
return—and the donor communication—must be documented.

Prior approval is therefore the normal and safest entry path, not a universal
rule that every newly proposed charitable purpose is automatically
nondeductible. The central questions are whether the gift benefits the qualified
organization rather than a specific individual, whether the organization
accepts the gift and its restriction, and whether the organization retains the
required control over administration of the funds. Unusual restricted gifts
should be reviewed with a qualified adviser.

### Required procedure for a person-specific direction

1. **Pause the contribution.** Do not include it on an ordinary charitable
   contribution statement while the direction remains unresolved.
2. **Speak with the giver.** Explain that the congregation must retain control
   and discretion and cannot accept the money merely as a conduit to the named
   person.
3. **Offer the approved purpose.** Ask whether the giver wishes to contribute to
   Student Support or another approved congregational purpose without requiring
   a particular recipient.
4. **Document the giver's decision.** Record the date, church representative,
   communication method, and a concise factual summary. Do not write a tax
   conclusion on the giver's behalf.
5. **Record or return it.** If the giver affirmatively removes the person-specific
   condition, record the gift for the approved purpose. If the giver retains the
   condition, return the payment or follow a separately reviewed
   non-contribution policy.
6. **Preserve the record.** Keep the clarification or return with the batch and
   directed-gift review history.

### What prior congregational approval accomplishes

Approval before accepting gifts is an important administrative safeguard. It
establishes that the purpose belongs to the congregation rather than being
invented as a conduit after a donor presents money for someone.

Prior approval alone is not enough. In actual practice the congregation must
also retain control and discretion. A fund named “Student Support” does not cure
a transaction if the donor still controls which student must receive the money.

### Approved-purpose records

For every purpose offered to donors, the treasurer should be able to identify:

- the approved purpose and its charitable relationship to the congregation;
- the approval date and approving body or authorized person;
- the active dates;
- the accounting fund and revenue mapping;
- who is authorized to decide recipients and distributions;
- confirmation that the congregation retains control and discretion; and
- the congregation's reviewed contribution-statement treatment.

ChurchManager will use these records to limit normal giving entry to active,
approved purposes and to flag person-specific instructions for review.

### Benevolence and emergency assistance

A genuine benevolence ministry normally uses congregation-established criteria
and an independent church decision about need, recipient, amount, and timing. A
donor may give to the benevolence ministry but should not control a payment to a
named person.

Benevolence cases can involve additional privacy, tax, employment, and private
benefit questions. Store recipient information only in the restricted process
that administers the assistance; do not place the recipient's identity on the
donor's contribution or ordinary statement.

### Contribution acknowledgments

Directed-gift review is separate from the rules governing acknowledgments. IRS
guidance describes the information required for written acknowledgments of
individual contributions of $250 or more and disclosures for certain payments
involving goods or services. See:

- [IRS: Charitable contributions—written acknowledgments](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/charitable-organizations/charitable-contributions-written-acknowledgments)
- [IRS Publication 1771, Charitable Contributions—Substantiation and Disclosure
  Requirements](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1771.pdf)

ChurchManager assists with these records and statements but does not determine
deductibility. The congregation remains responsible for its policies,
acknowledgment wording, and professional review.
